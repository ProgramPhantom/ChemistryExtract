## POLYDISPERSITY INDEX AND MOLECULAR WEIGHT DISTRIBUTIONS OF  POLYMERS

M.  ROGOSIC, H. J.  MENCER and Z. GOMZI

Faculty  of  Chemical Engineering  and Technology,  University of Zagreb,  MaruliCev  trg  20,  HR-IClOOO Zagreb,  Croatia

(Received  3  July 1995;  accepted in final  form 14  November 1995)

Abstract-The  polydispersity index  as  a  measure of  the  width  of  molecular  weight  distributions (MWD) is  theoretically very  important.  However, considering MWDs of  real polymers,  its importance  is  partly lost.  Consequently, several  problems  arise. The  most  common one  is the misinterpretation of polydispersity index  as  a single and unique measure of  the  MWD width.  In  this work, the  origin and solutions of  the  problem are  clearly described via  several theoretical distribution functions, i.e. those  which are  most widely  applied  for  the  description of polymer MWDs. The conclusions  are  confirmed by the analysis of MWDs of real  samples,  namely styrene-acrylonitrile copolymers.  In addition, a complete procedure for the MWD data analysis is  presented.  Such a procedure is  necessary  for  the  correct interpretation and comparison of  different, experimentally obtained,  molecular  weight  distributions of polymers. ## INTRODUCIION

As it  is  well  known,  the  incorporation  of  monomer units  into  polymer  molecules  during polymerization reaction  follows  certain statistical principles. Consequently, the product  of  such  reaction is never homogeneous at the  molecular level, i.e. molecular properties are  not constant  throughout the  polymer sample.  Therefore, while discussing a  molecular

property  of polymer, one has to keep in mind  the existence of  related distribution, which links  discrete property values with corresponding frequencies  of molecules  bearing  those  property  values. Depending on the  polymer type  [l],  a  sample may be polydispersive in  reference to  microstructure, degree of  chain  branching,  composition  of  copolymer, sequence of comonomer units in copolymer,  etc. Besides,  almost  every  polymer is polydispersive regarding  the  molecular  weight.  One  must remember that  specific properties of  the  polymeric  material  and the  polymeric  character itself are  determined,  among other  things, by the  unusually large  molecular size. Accordingly,  the  changes in  the  shape and width of molecular  weight  distributions are  directly reflected in the changes  of various properties. Therefore,  the basis of polymer  characterization has  to be  the determination  of  molecular  weight  distribution (MWD). Such  determinations are nowadays performed  routinely,  by  means of well-known size exclusion chromatography (SEC)  method.  Many excellent review  articles [24] describing  the  general features of  SEC  may  be  found in  the  literature. Great importance is  given to  the  interpretation  of experimental  data,  usually  with the  aid of modem computer simulation  techniques  [5-lo]. Still, the  way of  data  presentation and the  quality of  result interpretation often fall  behind  the possibilities of modem experimental  techniques, which results in  loss of  information.  In  this short  review, attention will be directed  towards  the unfortunately very common misinterpretation of  the  polydispersity index, which is used for  the  description of  the  MWD. Also,  a point will be  given  to  the procedures  that enable  a quantitative comparison of  different  molecular weight  distributions, and which should  be  performed in  every  MWD data analysis.

A  large  part of the following discussion  is  not necessarily restricted to  the  investigation of  polymer MWD, and some presented  solutions can be successfully transferred to other fields of chemical engineering, e.g. wherever a problem of  particle size distribution is  encountered.

## THEORETICAL DISTRIBUTION FUNCTIONS

In  most cases  the  discrete MWD may be approximated by  some suitable continuous  theoretical distribution function.  The  benefit of  those  approximations  is  apparent.  Theoretical  distribution functions,  which  are completely defined by  only  few adjustable  parameters,  may  be easily analyzed and later  built into the variety  of correlations. The commonly applied  theoretical distribution functions may be divided into two  main  groups. The  first contains  the  functions  developed exclusively for  the description of  polymer M WD,  using  simple  theoretical (statistical) considerations of  the  polymerization process  (e.g. Flory,  Schulz, lung, Pearson,  etc.). The other  includes well-known probability functions capable  ofdescribing the  real MWD (logarithmic-normal,  Poisson  function, etc.). All  mentioned functions are  unimodal, defined  for  positive arguments (degrees of polymerization),  and  asymmetric, i.e.  inclined towards the  lower  x-values. To  choose a proper function for  describing  the MWD of  a  polymeric  sample is not  an easy  task. The shape  of  the  molecular  weight  distribution  is determined mainly by the  mechanism  of  polymerization  reaction, and partly by the  characteristics of the polymer preparation process. Theoretical distribution functions  [12-161  are  derived  on the  basis  of statistical considerations for  the  idealized polymerization reactions. Consequently,  the experimentally observed deviations  from the  theory  may  imply the incorrectness of  the  theoretical postulates or  built-in approximations. The  theoretical fundamentals  of majority  of  developed  functions are  very  similar and, additionally,  the  mechanism of  reaction is not completely  known,  so  the  pre-choice of  the  function is  rarely possible. Besides, none of  the  functions  are especially simple or computationally suitable. It  is therefore best  to  take  into  account  several theoretical distribution functions  [17-191. For each  of  the  chosen functions, the  independent  fitting to  the  experimental data  should  be  performed  numerically, and  the optimal parameter values  should be computed.  To check the  validity of the  obtained fits, a numerical criterion should be postulated, e.g. the  mean-square deviation  of  the  experimental  and calculated values. On the basis of the defined criterion, the most adequate theoretical function  is  chosen. In further analysis, if  necessary, the polymer  MWD may  be replaced  by a given  function, of  course  with  proper parameter values.

## THE  POLYDISPERSITY INDEX:  PROBLEM, EXAMPLES AND  DISCUSSION

The  experimental determination of the polymer MWD is  routinely performed by the  gel-permeation chromatography (GPC) or  the  size-exclusion chromatography  (SEC) method. Nevertheless, the  qualitative information  on the  MWD width  may  be obtained  by direct determination of number and  weight MWD averages, usually  by the  membrane osmometry  and light scattering methods, respectively.

A ratio of  the  molecular  weight  averages, known  as the polydispersity index (heterogeneity ratio,  dispersion  ratio, nonuniformity coefficient), defined  by the  expression

$$I = \frac { \bar { x } _ { w } } { \bar { x } _ { \mathfrak { n } } } = \frac { \bar { M } _ { w } } { \bar { M } _ { 0 } }$$

is  commonly used for description  of the polymer MWD. The  wide variety  of the terms point out a possible misunderstanding  of  the  physical significance of  the  ratio. The polydispersity index  is often  taken  as the  absolute measure of  the  molecular  weight distribution, although some  objections  have  been presented  to  confront that  opinion [20]. The  higher polydispersity index  indeed  implies the  wider  MWD. However, contrary  to  the  widely  accepted  belief, the reverse is not  true, as  will be  illustrated below.

In  the  theory  of  the  probability functions, the  variance  of  distribution crj is normally used  as  the  measure of  the  distribution width.  The variance  of  the  MWD may  be  related to  the  corresponding  MWD averages:

$$\sigma _ { d } ^ { 2 } = \bar { x } _ { 0 } ^ { 2 } \left ( \frac { \bar { x } _ { w } } { \bar { x } _ { n } } - 1 \right ) .$$

Theoretical analysis of  the  polymer MWD usually involves  the  comparison of  the  distributions with  the same  number average molecular weight [ 121.  The narrowest  possible distribution, which  has  a  certain &amp;, is the monodispersive  one. All  averages of the monodispersive  polymer MWDs are  the same,  and the polydispersity index  is equal  to  1. The part  of  equation (43)  in parentheses  represents the  deviation  of the polydispersity index from  the  value of 1,  so every deviation (positive, because  &amp;  is always  larger than  or equal  to  .%,) is  reflected in  the  increase of  the  MWD width. Yet,  while  transferring the  theory into  practice, the  first part  of  expression  (43), i.e. the  square  of  the number  average  molecular weight,  is  often  ignored. Consequently, the increase  in the MWD width is automatically  interpreted in  terms  of  the  increase in the  polydispersity index. Contrary to the  common expectations, the  large increase  of the polydispersity index may  affect the distribution width  only slightly (Fig. 5). The  observed increase in the MWD width with  increasing content  of  acrylonitrile units in  the  copolymer is not  reflected in  the  change of the  polydispersity index,  which is  rather constant  for all  investigated copolymers.  To  quantify  the  dependence  of  the  distribution width  on the  composition of copolymers, the  MWDs of  investigated copolymers are described by  Schulz's  theoretical distribution function.

$$A = \int _ { \Omega } ^ { \zeta } B ( M ) d M$$

where A denotes the  area  and B(M) is  the  (experimentally determined)  relative frequency  of  the  molecules having  molecular  weight  M. The area  is calculated by the  convenient  numerical  method, e.g. trapezoidal or Simpson's rule.  As  a direct  consequence, wider distributions  appear  with  lower  ordinates,  and narrower MWDs have higher  ordinates. Afterwards the  previously chosen  theoretical distribution function may  be  fitted to  the  normalized  MWD data.

Before  any comparison of  the  various  MWDs, the processing of  the  'raw' distribution data  is needed.  It is  worthwhile  to  present here  the  entire procedure  of normalization,  as in practice  the normalization is either not  performed,  it is performed improperly, or  it is not  described and  hence  not  available  to an interested reader.

As  already  mentioned, the experimentally  determined MWDs contain  sets of  data  connecting individual  molecular weights (abscissae) with the corresponding relative frequencies  (ordinates). The first normalization  (normalization no.  1) is performed in order to determine the best-fitting theoretical function, and it comprises  the  normalization  of  both the abscissae  and  the ordinates.  The  goal of the abscissae  normalization is to simplify the fitting procedure,  by  avoiding large  numbers (molecular weights)  in numerical  operations.  Division of all abscissae of  all distributions by the  same, arbitrarily chosen value  brings  them into  the  range  of  relatively low numbers. The first normalization  of  ordinates is performed by dividing  all  ordinates  of a particular distribution by the  corresponding  maximal  ordinate. Consequently, the  chosen criteria for  the  success  of data  fitting  (usually mean-square deviations of experimental and calculated data) become comparable values  for  all experimental  distributions.  The theoretical distribution function  capable  of  describing all experimental  MWDs is chosen on the  basis of  the criterion values computed during the fitting procedure.

If the  objects of  the comparison are  the  distribution shapes  (normalization no.  3), disregarding the absolute  width, then the abscissae  of a particular distribution should  be  divided by  some of  the  averages, e.g. by the  number  average  molecular  weight. In  order to  avoid  the  ambiguity  of  the  interpretation, it is again very  important  to  stress  the chosen  norm. All ordinates of  a  particular distribution should  be  divided by the  corresponding maximal  ordinate. Hence  the ordinate heights become comparable,  and the  eventual variations in  shape  are  more explicit. This  normalization  procedure may  be followed  by the  convenient fitting procedure  as  well.
