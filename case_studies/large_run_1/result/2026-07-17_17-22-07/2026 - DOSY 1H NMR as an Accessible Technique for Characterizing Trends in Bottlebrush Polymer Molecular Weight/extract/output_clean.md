ABSTRACT: Bottlebrush polymers (BBPs) make up a distinctive class of graft copolymers with densely packed side chains, whose unusual macromolecular architecture gives rise to unique physical properties with broad relevance across scientific disciplines. However, their branched architecture challenges traditional molecular weight characterization techniques. Gel permeation chromatography with multiangle light scattering (GPC-MALS) can accurately account for their branching, but this technique is not universally available. ## ■ INTRODUCTION

Bottlebrush polymers (BBPs) and bottlebrush block copolymers (BBCPs) make up an intriguing class of graft copolymers characterized by densely grafted side chains covalently attached to a central backbone. Their unique architecture, reminiscent of a plant of the same name, introduces a range of interesting properties and molecular parameters not found in conventional linear polymers. Thes roperties include significant suppression of entanglements, , rapid and well-ordered selfs bly over an impressive range of sizes and morphologies, -and sterically induced conformational changes that have dramatic impacts for their ap c ions as elastomers, photonic materials and pigments, -compatibilizers, and nanostructure templates. As a result, BBPs have gained significant attention in recent years, with interest likely to continue as new applications and chemistries emerge. However, the same molecular architecture that distinguishes BBPs from their linear counterparts also creates challenges for their characterization, specifically determining their molecular weights and solution conformations.

BBPs and BBCPs are commonly synthesized via grafting through polymerization of 'macromonomers,' or low-molecular-weight polymers (&lt;10 kDa) bearing a polymerizable functional group ( i.e. , norbornene or vinyl groups) that form the chemical backbone once polymerized. Similarly, since the backbone and end-group typically make up a small percentage of the total weight of the polymer, end-group analysis via Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy (NMR) is challenging, as these signals become dominated by the signals of side chains (see ). While light scattering techniques allow for accurate M w determination, they require specialized equipment and numerous data points at various concentrations, increasing analysis time. Viscometric techniques, although successfully deriving relationships involving M w for select bottlebrush systems, typically require large quantities of the polymer of interest and prove complex for phase-segregated BBCPs.

Traditional gel permeation chromatography (GPC), the most common method for polymer M w determination, requires additional considerations for BBPs as detailed by Walsh et al. For BBPs of sufficient length, the dispersity measured in GPC is only characteristic of the backbone distribution. Thus, when side chains are grafted from or to the backbone, GPC does not provide information regarding grafting density or side-chain M w distribution. Additionally, for BBCPs, monomodality is not sufficient to ensure the purity of the produced polymer, as factors such as refractive index differences, hydrodynamic scaling, and the separation resolution play important roles. Thus, researchers must often resort to GPC techn u equipped with multiangle light scattering (GPC-MALS), -which combines light scattering principles with size-exclusion chromatography. While GPC-MALS accurately determines molecular weight distributions (MWDs) for branched architectures, its high cost can create a significant barrier to entry in BBP research.

Diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) is a two-dimensional NMR technique commonly performed using standard NMR spectrometers, which are ubiquitous among chemistry institutions. Pointedly, DOSY can even be performed with high accuracy on low-field, low-power benchtop spectrometers. DOSY and GPC both rely upon determining a polymer's hydrodynamic radius ( R h ) in solution, contrasting static light scattering techniques which rely upon the polymer radius of gyration ( R g). The R h of a polymer provides a measure of size in terms of hydrodynamic drag and is often inferred from the Stokes -Einstein equation. This relationship equates both the polymer and the solvent trapped within the coil to an impermeable sphere that has the same center-of-mass diffusion ( b). Thus, as polymer M w increases, its R h increases and the polymer experiences more drag ( i.e. , it diffuses more slowly). We can probe this change by measuring the polymer's effective diffusion coefficient ( D ) by DOSY. The relationship between M w and R h (or D -1 ) has b n xplored in thorough detail for linear polymer systems -allowing researchers to determine both molecular weight and polymerization kinetics with high fidelity.

Despite the challenges in determining BBP molecular weight and the widespread accessibility of DOSY, there has not been a systematic report of its use for this purpose. A key advantage of DOSY is its solvent versatility  it can be performed in virtually any solvent in small amounts, provided there is sufficient gradient strength to suppress the signal of nondeuterated solvents. GPC generally requires large solvent volumes for column washing and chromatogram acquisition. Notably, DOSY has also demonstrated high accuracy in determining the M w of linear polymers with low-field (80 MHz) benchtop NMR spectrometers. Furthermore, because NMR spectroscopy is already used to characterize polymer structure, the same sample can also be used for DOSY to determine molecular weight distribution, saving material and time.

Here, we demonstrate its utility through the synthesis of a 17-member BBP and BBCP library varying in molecular weight, composition, and architecture using PDMS and PEG side-chain chemistries. DOSY experiments were performed to obtain diffusion coefficients, which were then correlated with molecular weights determined by GPC-MALS. Linearized models relating hydrodynamic radius ( R h) to known M w values exhibited strong correlation ( R &gt;0.98) across all architectures tested. These models also reveal scaling behavior that offers insights into the complex solution-state properties of the BBPs. This study highlights the potential of DOSY as an accessible and effective technique for BBP and BBCP molecular weight characterization.

Following polymerization, all samples were subjected to MALS-GPC to evaluate M w, followed by DOSY at 1 mg/mL in CDCl3 by utilizing a Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. The 1 H resonance frequency was set to 400.13 MHz in a 5 mm broadband-observe Prodigy cryoprobe, 3 mm NMR tubes, a probe temperature of 293 K, and a stimulated echo pulse sequence with a bipolar gradient. The methyl 1 H signal of the PDMS side chains (0.09 ppm) was used to track diffusive behavior, as this signal is present on all polymer samples and is notably sharp/distinct. All signal decay curves were fit using the Bruker Dynamics Center software, and an example analysis can be found in the Supporting Information ( ). Specifically, a two-component fit was used to negate diffusive effects from residual unfunctionalized PDMS or PEG present following the esterification step. We note that care should be taken such that DOSY samples are at concentrations lower than the overlap concentration of synthesized bottlebrushes. As described models and simulations by Borisov and co-workers, , bottlebrush polymers in dilute solution exhibit a persistence length that scales linearly with the bottlebrush cross section, allowing the estimation of a sample's R g (and thus overlap concentration) from variables such as side chain length and grafting density. Utilizing these models shows good agreement with the measured values of R h . The reader is encouraged to refer to for details on the equations utilized.

## ■ SCALING RELATIONSHIPS AND SOLVENT QUALITY

Before investigating the bottlebrush polymer molecular weights by DOSY, it is important to understand the information that the derived scaling relationships provide. Namely, the scaling relationships seen in these types of experiments can inform us about the 'quality' of solvent used in these systems as they pertain to enthalpic interactions. Theta solvents are described as solvents of sufficient quality such that two-body interactions ( i.e. , monomer -monomer-excluded me interactions) appear to be screened, or nonexistent. , This means that the polymer's characteristic length ( R g, R h ) scales with the molecular weight to the 0.50 power. Importantly, though, theta solvents are not enthalpically neutral to the polymer  there still lies some, albeit small, penalty for having the pol chain surrounded by the solvent molecules ( i.e. , χ ≠ 0). ,

When the polymer samples have a solvent to which it is truly enthalpically neutral (meaning that the monomers in the polymer chain have no preference of having an adjacent monomer or a solvent molecule next to it), the polymer is in an a thermal solvent. Thus, the polymer will attempt to form a truly random walk, but since monomer units cannot occupy the same space, the polymer chain is slightly swollen, meaning that it will scale with M w to 0.588, better known as the Flory exponent. These distinctions are important to note as the bottlebrush architecture introduces additional entropic considerations not found in linear polymers, meaning that scaling relationships derived for linear polymers cannot necessarily be directly translated to their bottlebrush counterparts.

## ■ SELF-COILING PHENOMENA

The scaling relationships, as determined by the slopes of the different polymer systems, provide insights into complex solution-state bottlebrush physics. In the case of the NPDMS macromonomers, the polymer coils can freely diffuse throughout the solvent, allowing a slightly smaller-than-ideal polymer chain conformation as seen by the slope of 0.52. However, once these macromonomers are tethered to a central backbone, as in the case of the PDMS homopolymer BBPs, the slope changes dramatically. Once polymerized, the side chains experience a reduction in their conformational degrees of freedom as steric effects from adjacent side chains and covalent attachment to the backbone prevent the adoption of their isolated, single-chain conformation. Naturally, the high grafting density also means that PDMS side chains remain closely packed to one another once they are stitched together via the norbornene backbone. We hypothesize that this proximity and the reduction in degrees of freedom render the solvent, which is slightly enthalpically unfavorable to PDMS (as seen by scaling behavior lower than a true self-avoiding walk in

a), to screen the interactions between adjacent side chains ( ). As a result, the polymer will prefer a more globular conformation, as seen by the reduction in the scaling relationship between R h and M w to 0.36 and 0.38 for the 1 and 5 kDa BBPs, respectively ( ). For reference, the R h of an impermeable sphere with a constant monomer density would scale as M w ∝ 0.33. We refer to this behavior as 'selfcoiling,' as the scaling behavior suggests that the side chains aggregate and collapse in on themselves despite the high conformational penalty to do so. These results differ but are not mutually exclusive from those published by Dutta et al., who characterized the solutionstate behavior of poly[norbornenegraft -poly(lactic acid)] bottlebrush homopolymers in chlorobenzene, using dynamic light scattering. Dutta et al. found that the R h of their BBPs with varying side chain lengths scaled with M w to 0.58 and 0.56 power for 5.2 and 2.3 kDa side chains, respectively. However, the increase in their observed scaling behavior can be attributed to the quality of the solvent, as the authors note that chlorobenzene is an athermal solvent for the poly(lactic) side chains. Thus, the tendency of their side chains to self-coil would be absent, leading to a more swollen coil conformation and an increased scaling exponent. Reduced scaling relationships similar to those seen in our work have also been observed by Leo et al. in poly[norbornenegraft -poly(styrene)] BBPs in dilute solution and in multiple other systems as highlighted by Sunday et al. Generally, the observed scaling phenomena of bottlebrush polymers can be difficult to derive theoretically due to compounding and complex interactions. Foundational works by Birshtein predicted a scaling exponent of 3/5 for bottlebrush polymers in a theta solvent, differing than the generally lower-observed values in the literature. This likely stems from assumptions of equal and negligible enthalpic interactions between the solvent and the polymer side chains and backbone, in addition to neglecting any enthalpic interactions between the side chains and backbone itself.

These important considerations are further complicated with the introduction of an additional side chain identity, as in the case with copolymers. Here, one must consider nonuniform swelling of the side chains, additional side chain -side chain enthalpic interactions (as in the case with our PEG -PDMS systems), and how all of these behaviors may change the effective 'stiffness' or 'blob size'.

Despite a similar scaling behavior for both the 1 and 5 kDa BBPs ( ), the R h measured via DOSY is notably different for a given n bb ( c). We believe that these differences are attributed to steric constraints imposed by the longer, 5 kDa PDMS side chains. While both systems exhibit a tendency toward more collapsed conformations, the increase in side chain length may slightly hinder a complete collapse of the polymeric backbone into a sphere. Naturally, since the side chains appear to be dominating the conformation of the polymer, having larger side chains leads to a proportionally larger globule. Additionally, any 5 kDa PDMS side chains that exist outside of the globule make the polymer exhibit more drag, increasing R h. These differences are most prominent when you compare PDMS BBPs of similar n bb ( c). However, plotting the M w (importantly not n bb ) vs R h for all PDMS BBPs shows that the two homopolymers may exist on the same curve ( ). Naturally, more side chain lengths should be tested, but this result suggests that the diffusive behavior of homopolymer bottlebrushes is dominated by the cumulative weight of the side chains, independent of the length of an individual side chain. Further, these relationships for homopolymer BBPs could be used to construct a 'calibration curve', allowing researchers to estimate M w solely from the measured diffusion coefficient irrespective of n sc for a given side chain/solvent system. This apparent insensitivity to side chain length has also been seen in dynamic light scattering measurements by Dutta et al.

To investigate this phenomenon further, we introduced chemically dissimilar PEG side chains, which are known to unfav e ct and phase s e p a r a t e with PDMS. , , , , , -These PEG side chains, due to their proximity to the PDMS side chains, were anticipated to prevent the collapse of BBCP into dense spheres. Specifically, placing these immiscible PDMS and PEG side chains next to one another should impose an enthalpic penalty too high for the phenomenon to occur. Thus, the backbone will favor coil expansion over collapse, increasing the scaling exponent observed as the chains swell, and these interactions begin to be screened.

Upon introduction of the PEG side chains in the BBCPs, we observed a noticeable increase in the scaling behavior (or slope) from 0.38 to 0.45 for the 5 kDa PDMS BBPs and 5 kDa PEG/PDMS BBCPs, respectively ( ). This suggests that even though the PEG side chains only lie next to PDMS side chains at the interface between the two blocks, their inclusion is enough to alter the observed scaling behavior toward an expanded coil conformation. We similarly expected that increasing the number of PEG side chains near the PDMS side chains in a random copolymer architecture would cause an even higher degree of swelling. Accordingly, despite having the shortest side chain lengths, we observed the highest slope ( m = 0.48) for the BSCPs ( ). The absolute hydrodynamic size of these BSCPs was reduced compared to their block copolymer counterparts, as the PEG and PDMS side chains are substantially shorter (1 and 2 kDa versus 5 and 5 kDa, respectively). It is likely, then, that a BSCP with 5 kDa side chains would have a larger R h than the same side chains in a block configuration.

## ■ CONCLUSIONS

We have shown that DOSY and GPC-MALS can be successfully employed as molecular weight determination methods for a diverse set of bottlebrush polymers. Even for polymers with significant variations in side chain length, composition, and synthetic approaches, DOSY reliably predicted the molecular weight, producing linear calibration curves. We propose that differences in the scaling behavior between these samples arise from a self-coiling phenomenon, where the proximity of chemically similar side chains results in a more collapsed conformation, as side chain -side chain interactions may not be able to be screened by the solvent. This effect, however, can be mitigated by introducing chemically dissimilar macromonomers that disrupt self-coiling through unfavorable interactions, leading to coil expansion. Despite the broad range of structural differences among the polymer series, our findings demonstrate that DOSY is a reliable technique for determining both the M w trends of and solution behavior for bottlebrush polymers. These results additionally emphasize DOSY's versatility and effectiveness in characterizing complex polymer architectures and helps democratize future research into this fascinating class of macromolecules. These results emphasize the potential of DOSY in the characterization of complex polymer architectures, encouraging future work to examine other difficult-tocharacterize molecules such as zwitterions, complex coacervates, or simple sugars.
