## 1. Introduction

Pulse Field Gradient Spin Echo (PFGSE) Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) experiments are a type of NMR methodology used for quantitative analysis of molecular diffusion. This technique powers the ability to spatially encode the diffusion of molecules through the application of a pulse field gradient. PGSE NMR is often combined with other NMR methods to provide detailed information about the structure and dynamics of molecules in a sample. It is widely used in chemistry, biology, and materials science because it can provide valuable insights into the size, shape, molecular weight, and interactions of molecules with other substances. In the last years, there has been particular interest in using this methodology to determine the molecular weight of polymers. .

Diffusion NMR is a widely used technique to measure diffusion coefficients, which provides insights into the size and mobility of molecules in solution. For polymer and other systems, these diffusion coefficients are often linked to  molecular weight through empirical scaling relationships, allowing  the molecular weight to be estimated indirectly. However, factors such as solvent type and polymer concentration can have a significant impact on the precision of molecular weight predictions derived from these coefficients. Typically, diffusion NMR measurements are performed at very low concentrations to minimize distortions in diffusion coefficients, which can affect molecular weight estimation. However, this approach poses challenges, such as inaccuracies due to the need to very small quantities of polymer. Additionally, achieving reliable results often requires numerous scans and long recovery periods between scans to account for T1 relaxation times, leading to extended experimental durations. ## 2.4. Method for molecular weight calculation

In this section, we describe the methodology of the iterative method used  to  determine  the  molecular  weight  of  polymers  based  on  their diffusion coefficients and solvent viscosity, independent of concentration  and  solvent  type.  This  method  is  grounded  on  the  previously described Eq. (9), Eqs. (11) and (12), allowing for molecular weight determination across a wide range of polymer concentrations and solvent  types,  circumventing  the  need  for  highly  diluted  samples  traditionally required in traditional diffusion NNR technique.

The procedure is an iterative process which starts with an initial assumption for the molecular weight, typically using a low-value estimation of 2 Da. The D η | c is then calculated at a specific polymer concentration.  Using  Eqs. ,  the  weight-average  molecular weight  Mw  is  iteratively  refined  by  calculating  new D η | * c based  on updated values for the molecular weight and comparing them with the experimentally measured values. If a significant difference between the calculated and measured values exists, the molecular weight is adjusted, and the process is repeated until convergence is achieved. This entire procedure is highly efficient, taking less than 200 ms per sample (see

below).

The  iterative  process  was  implemented  using  a  combination  of advanced  computational  tools.  Matlab ® scripts  were  developed  to execute the routine, as provided in Code S1 and Code S2 for the two polymer systems tested. Diffusion coefficients were obtained using the DiffAtOnce (LMS) software, written in C#, and refined with the dART and TRAIn algorithms, both of which were coded in Matlab ® .  DOSY experiments were processed using the alternating direction sparse and low-rank unmixing (ADSpLRU) algorithm, also coded in Matlab ® . These tools  ensure  robust  diffusion  coefficient  estimarions,  facilitating  the high-precision  calculations  necessary  for  accurate  molecular  weight determination.

In all cases, the methodology described yielded average error rates below 4 %, underscoring its accuracy and consistency in determining molecular weights across various polymer types and solvent environments. This conservative estimation highlights the reliability of diffusion NMR as a complementary technique to SEC. The molecular weights obtained using this methodology closely align with those determined by SEC ( ), with deviations typically within 1 -4 %. These differences can be attributed to the inherent variations in the principles underlying the  two  techniques:  SEC  relies  on  calibration  standards  and  column separations, while diffusion NMR directly measures molecular motion in solution. SEC provides detailed molecular weight distributions and is ideal for routine analysis but requires calibration standards, consumables,  and  regular  maintenance,  making  it  less  suitable  for  complex polymers  or  solvent-sensitive  systems.  Diffusion  NMR,  on  the  other hand,  uses  diffusion  coefficients  to  infer  molecular  weight  without external calibration and excels in solvent flexibility and complex polymer analysis. Although the dataset focuses on a subset of the polymers listed  in ,  this  selection  represents  a  proof  of  concept  for  the diffusion NMR methodology. The chosen polymers span a wide range of molecular weights and were tested in multiple solvent environments and different  concentrations  to  validate  the  applicability  of  the  method under diverse conditions. Recent advancements, such as ultrafast (UF) and  time-resolved  (TR)  diffusion  NMR  methods,  have  significantly reduced acquisition times, making the technique more competitive with SEC in terms of speed. Ongoing efforts are focused on adapting these methods for faster and more accurate polymer molecular weight predictions,  potentially  bridging  the  gap  between  the  two  techniques. Future work will expand the dataset to include additional polymers, further generalizing the approach and solidifying its role as a practical and efficient tool for polymer analysis.

## 4. Conclusions

An innovative iterative methodology for accurately estimating the F.M. Arrabal-Campos et al.

molecular weight of linear and narrow PPG and PS polymers has been described for the first time using both PGSE and SILT-DOSY NMR procedures. The main inputs to this method are the quantitative diffusion coefficient (D), solvent viscosity, and sample concentration. The method has been demonstrated to be valid in a wide range of concentrations, thanks to the developed Kappa and high dilution curves. These curves capture the growing polymeric interactions that occur as a function of concentration,  helping  to  deduce  true  molecular  weight  at  any  concentration and in any deuterated solvent. The molecular weight predictions obtained by this method has achieved an absolute error below 4 % in all the cases, indicating its high accuracy and effectiveness. This new iterative method is presented in the as Code

S1 and Code S2. Current research efforts are focused on exploring the broader  application  of  this  method  to  other  polymers  with  higher polydispersity  and  especially  those  based  on  polysaccharides.  Additionally,  we  are  investigating  the  implications  of  the  transitions observed in the Kappa plot, which likely reflect shifts in polymer dynamics  due  to  excluded  volume  effects  and  mean-field  interactions. These transitions hold potential for providing deeper insights into chain behavior, entanglements, and polymer -solvent interactions.
